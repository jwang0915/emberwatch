Emberwatch · James Wang, West Point Grey Academy

Where should British Columbia put wildfire detection drones?

Emberwatch combines a machine learning forecast of where wildfires start with an integer program that chooses drone base sites, then replays real fire seasons to see what the network would deliver. Every choice on this page was made using data up to the end of 2021 and scored against the wildfires that actually started in 2022 to 2025.

Distance is not the same as being there

The replay sends a drone to every 2022–25 wildfire, one at a time, on the day it started. Drones get busy, 15% of calls are grounded by weather, and lightning storms start dozens of fires in an afternoon. Coverage drawn on a map overstates what the network delivers.

What the risk model learned

A gradient-boosted model with a Poisson loss predicts how many wildfires will start in each 10 km cell next season. It only sees years before the one it predicts. Trained on 2010–19, tuned on 2020–21, tested on 2022–25.

What it leans on

Share of the model's total gain from each input. Past fires dominate; roads and recent burns add a little.

How it scores against history

One season ahead, 2022–25 pooled. "Top 10% of cells" is the share of next season's fires that land in the 10% of cells ranked riskiest.

The 2023 stress test

2023 was BC’s record season, so the model was retrained as if it were 31 December 2022 and then scored on what happened. It forecast 1,449 wildfires against 2,296 real ones. It was close in the south and far off in the north, where fires had been rare.

Drought, weather and moving bases

To try to fix the 2023 miss, monthly temperature, precipitation, soil moisture and snow depth for 479 points were added from NASA POWER, each as an anomaly from that point’s own 1999 to 2019 normal. Drought known before the season did not predict BC fire seasons. Moving the bases each month helped more than any forecast.

Moving monthly beat the fixed network in 15 of 20 months. The weather adjustment added about one point on top of moving, which may be noise. Pre-season drought did not predict BC fire seasons, and summer rain, which nobody knows in May, did.

Assumptions to argue with

No real product is being modelled. These numbers describe a long-range fixed-wing drone that takes off and lands vertically from a charging dock. Change them in src/config.py and rerun.

  • The BC records give a date for each fire, not a time. Report times are drawn from an afternoon-heavy spread.
  • Weather grounding is random here. In reality the storms that start fires also keep drones down, so the replay is optimistic on lightning days.
  • Sites are limited to BC Wildfire Service weather stations and towns. Airspace, terrain, road access and Indigenous land rights are not modelled.
  • Since November 2025, Transport Canada allows flights beyond visual line of sight only in lower-risk conditions: uncontrolled airspace, below 122 m, away from aerodromes, with a Level 1 Complex pilot certificate. Anything else needs a special flight operations certificate. Staffing and cost are not modelled.
  • Fire records include fires that were deliberately monitored rather than fought, so some large fires were large by choice.
